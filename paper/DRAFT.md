## 1. Introduction

Rydberg atom arrays are a programmable quantum simulation platform in which individual atoms are trapped in optical tweezers, arranged atom by atom into chosen one- and two-dimensional geometries, and laser-excited to Rydberg states of high principal quantum number [1–3]. Because Rydberg atoms have large electronic orbitals, they interact strongly over micrometer distances through long-range van der Waals interactions, whose coefficient $C_6$ grows approximately as the eleventh power of the principal quantum number [4,5]. By controlling laser parameters and atom geometry, researchers can simulate interacting quantum many-body systems and investigate quantum phase transitions, ordered phases, frustrated magnetism and topological phases of matter. Because the interaction geometry and Hamiltonian parameters are tuned experimentally, theoretical models can be compared directly with quantum simulations [1].

The standard model for a Rydberg atom array is

$$
H=\frac{\hbar\Omega}{2}\sum_i\sigma_i^x-\hbar\Delta\sum_i n_i+\sum_{i<j}\frac{C_6}{r_{ij}^6}\,n_in_j, \qquad (1)
$$

where $\sigma_i^x=|g_i\rangle\langle r_i|+|r_i\rangle\langle g_i|$ drives transitions between the ground state $|g\rangle$ and the Rydberg state $|r\rangle$ of atom $i$ at the Rabi frequency $\Omega$, $n_i=|r_i\rangle\langle r_i|$ counts Rydberg excitations, $\Delta$ is the laser detuning and $r_{ij}$ is the distance between atoms $i$ and $j$ [1]. The detuning acts as a chemical potential: for $\Delta>0$ each excitation lowers the energy by $\hbar\Delta$, while the last term penalizes excitations that are close together. When $C_6/r^6\gg\hbar\Omega$, the doubly excited state of two atoms is shifted out of resonance and only one of them can be excited. This Rydberg blockade between two individually trapped atoms was first observed in 2009 [6,7]. The blockade radius $R_b$, defined by $C_6/R_b^6=\hbar\Omega$, sets the distance within which simultaneous excitation is suppressed [1]. For a chain with spacing $a$, the physics is controlled by $\Delta/\Omega$ and the dimensionless coupling constant $g=C_6/(\hbar\Omega a^6)=(R_b/a)^6$. Mapping the resulting phase diagrams requires solving a quantum many-body problem, usually by exact diagonalization (ED) for small systems or by tensor-network methods such as the density-matrix renormalization group (DMRG) for larger ones [8,9].

**Phases in one and two dimensions.** In a uniform chain at positive detunings of a few $\Omega$, too small to overcome the blockade, the excitations crystallize: every second atom is excited in the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ phase, every third in the $\mathbb{Z}_3$ phase and every fourth in the $\mathbb{Z}_4$ phase, for $R_b/a$ roughly between 1 and 2, 2 and 3, and 3 and 4, respectively [1,10]. Bernien et al. prepared these crystals in chains of up to 51 atoms by sweeping the detuning [1], and Keesling et al. used the quantum Kibble–Zurek mechanism to probe the critical dynamics of the transitions into them [10]. The transitions differ in kind [11–15]. Melting of the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ crystal is in the Ising universality class, whose conformal-field-theory fields have been identified with microscopic operators of Rydberg chains [15]. Three-state Potts criticality occurs only at one commensurate point of the $\mathbb{Z}_3$ boundary; away from such special points the $\mathbb{Z}_3$ and $\mathbb{Z}_4$ crystals melt through chiral transitions of Huse–Fisher type or through an intermediate incommensurate floating phase [11–14], which has since been probed experimentally [16]. In two dimensions, Ebadi et al. observed checkerboard, striated and star-ordered phases on a 256-atom square array [17], and Scholl et al. studied antiferromagnetic ordering on square and triangular arrays of up to hundreds of atoms [18]. Verresen, Lukin and Vishwanath predicted that the blockade constraint on the ruby lattice stabilizes a $\mathbb{Z}_2$ topological spin liquid of the toric-code type [19], and Semeghini et al. detected signatures of this state using topological string operators [20].

**Rydberg arrays for quantum computing.** Besides analog Hamiltonian simulation [17], the blockade makes Rydberg arrays a platform for digital quantum computing. Jaksch et al. proposed fast controlled-phase gates between neutral atoms based on Rydberg interactions [21], and Lukin et al. showed how dipole blockade enables quantum information processing in atomic ensembles [22]. The Lukin group and collaborators then implemented parallel multiqubit gates, including a three-qubit Toffoli gate [23], reached two-qubit gate fidelities of 99.5% in parallel across 60 atoms [24], and used coherent transport of entangled atoms to obtain reconfigurable, non-local connectivity [25]. This architecture underlies a logical quantum processor with up to 48 logical qubits [26]. Because the blockade constraint is the independent-set condition on a unit-disk graph, the arrays also encode combinatorial optimization directly, as in the study of the maximum independent set problem on graphs of up to 289 atoms [27].

**Why alternate the spacing?** Tweezers can place atoms with alternating short and long gaps, down to a minimum spacing set by the hardware. Bond alternation is the defining ingredient of the Su–Schrieffer–Heeger (SSH) model [28], which de Léséleuc et al. realized for interacting bosons using dipolar exchange between two Rydberg states, observing a symmetry-protected topological phase [29]. Zeybek, Mukherjee and Schmelcher found a bond-order density-wave phase in a number-conserving dimerized chain with van der Waals and dipolar exchange interactions [30]; related phases occur in dimerized extended Bose–Hubbard models [31]. Other structured chains alternate the atomic species [32], and multicomponent arrays have been proposed as a way to tune quantum criticality [33].

## Project direction

In the uniform Rydberg chain, the crystal that forms at large detuning changes from $\mathbb{Z}_2$ to $\mathbb{Z}_3$ and $\mathbb{Z}_4$ as the blockade radius $R_b = (C_6/\Omega)^{1/6}$ grows relative to the spacing $a$ [1]. The transitions out of these crystals include Ising, three-state Potts and chiral transitions, as well as intervening floating phases [10–12]. Tweezer arrays place each atom independently, so the spacing itself is a control parameter. This project asks: *how does geometric dimerization, with alternating gaps $s = a(1-\varepsilon)$ and $w = a(1+\varepsilon)$, reshape the ground-state phase diagram of the single-level, laser-driven van der Waals chain, and can one pair pseudo-spin rule predict where each crystal melts?* Dimerization keeps the form of the Hamiltonian and the atomic species. It changes the couplings and lowers the symmetry: translation by one atom is lost, and what remains is translation by one pair, $T_2$, and inversion about the centre of a strong bond, $I_s$.

The pseudo-spin picture makes three predictions, which we test against numerically exact results. First, every crystal whose occupied pairs carry a free left/right choice must lose its left/right order at some $\varepsilon < 1$ for fixed $\Delta$ and $R_b/a$. Second, even-period crystals acquire an additional transition, whereas odd-period crystals do not. Third, dimerization alone should not make the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ melting tricritical.

### 2.1 Model, units and geometry

We set $\hbar=1$ and measure energies in units of the Rabi frequency $\Omega$, lengths in units of the mean interatomic spacing $a$, and time as $\Omega t$. Equation (1) then becomes

$$\frac{H}{\Omega}=\frac12\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}X_i-\delta\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}n_i+\sum_{i<j}V_{ij}\,n_in_j,\qquad V_{ij}=\frac{g}{\lvert x_i-x_j\rvert^{6}},\qquad (2)$$

$$\delta=\frac{\Delta}{\Omega},\qquad g=\frac{C_6}{\Omega a^6}=\left(\frac{R_b}{a}\right)^{6},\qquad (3)$$

where $X_i=\sigma^x_i$, $n_i=|1\rangle\langle1|_i$ projects atom $i$ onto the Rydberg state, the positions $x_i$ are in units of $a$, and $R_b=(C_6/\Omega)^{1/6}$ is the blockade radius, the distance at which the van der Waals energy equals $\Omega$ [1,4]. Each pair is counted once. The dimerized-chain calculations use mainly $g=11.39$, 387.4 and 1291.5, that is, $R_b/a=1.50$, 2.70 and 3.30, which select the $\mathbb Z_2$, $\mathbb Z_3$ and $\mathbb Z_4$ regimes (Sec. 3.1); a few use $g=4.83$ ($R_b/a=1.30$), and the historical uniform-chain scans use $g=2$, 8 and 16<!-- source: 1.5^6 = 11.39, 2.7^6 = 387.42, 3.3^6 = 1291.47, 1.3^6 = 4.83; values as used in research/pilots/2026-09-27/outputs/crit_*.json, z3_24_*.txt and out24_*.txt; g = 2, 8, 16 from study/results/extension_summary.json grid.g. Checker, 27 Sept: the earlier sentence listed only the first three values, but 04_results_alternating.md also uses g = 4.83 and 05_results_size.md uses g = 2, 8, 16. -->.

**Dimerized geometry.** The chain has alternating gaps,

$$x_0=0,\qquad x_{i+1}-x_i=\begin{cases}1-\varepsilon, & i\ \text{even (strong bond)},\\ 1+\varepsilon, & i\ \text{odd (weak bond)},\end{cases}\qquad 0\le\varepsilon<1.\qquad (4)$$

Atoms $2k$ and $2k+1$ form strong pair $k$, located at $x=2k$ and $2k+s$ with $s=1-\varepsilon$. Because a strong and a weak gap always sum to $2$, the mean spacing, and with it the meaning of $g$, does not depend on $\varepsilon$; $\varepsilon=0$ is the uniform chain. The nearest-neighbour energies are $V_s=g/(1-\varepsilon)^6$ inside a pair and $V_w=g/(1+\varepsilon)^6$ between pairs. An open chain with even $N$ consists of $N/2$ complete pairs and starts and ends with a strong bond.

All pairs of atoms are retained unless a cutoff is stated. Truncated models keep couplings with index separation $\lvert i-j\rvert\le r_{\max}$ (atom sites) or pair separation $\le m_{\max}$ (pair sites, Sec. 2.3). Periodic rings (Sec. 2.2) use the minimum-image distance in the pair-basis scripts and a sum over the images $x_j+mN$, $\lvert m\rvert\le3$, in the constrained-basis script.

**A bound on interaction cutoffs.** Because every $V_{ij}$ is positive, discarding a set $\mathcal D$ of pairs removes the diagonal, positive-semidefinite operator $D=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal D}V_{ij}n_in_j$, whose largest eigenvalue is $\lVert D\rVert=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal D}V_{ij}$ (all atoms excited). Weyl's inequality [41] then bounds every ordered eigenvalue,

$$E_k^{\text{trunc}}\le E_k\le E_k^{\text{trunc}}+\lVert D\rVert,\qquad (5)$$

so no energy or gap changes by more than $\lVert D\rVert$. The bound says nothing about densities or correlations, which can change more when two levels are close. For the pair-site cutoff $m_{\max}=8$ in a 20-atom chain with $\varepsilon=0.3$, $g=11.39$, the bound is $\lVert D\rVert=1.4\times10^{-6}$<!-- source: scratch check, 27 Sept: sum of V_ij over atom pairs in pairs more than 8 apart, from rydberg_chain.geometry.coupling_matrix(chain_positions(20, 0.3), 11.39) = 1.361e-6 -->, and the ground-state energy actually shifts by $2.4\times10^{-7}$<!-- source: validation/results/scaling_benchmark.json, dmrg_pair entry N = 20, mmax = 8: E0_minus_ED = -2.44e-7 -->.

**Basis and bit convention.** We use the occupation basis $|b\rangle$ labelled by the integer $b=\sum_ib_i2^i$, where $b_i\in\{0,1\}$ is the occupation of atom $i$, so atom 0 is the least significant bit. This is Qiskit's little-endian qubit order: in a printed Qiskit label atom 0 is the rightmost character. With $|0\rangle$ the atomic ground state and $Z|0\rangle=+|0\rangle$,

$$n_i=\tfrac12\,(I-Z_i).\qquad (6)$$

### 2.2 Exact diagonalization

**Matrix elements.** The Hilbert space of $N$ atoms has dimension $2^N$. In the occupation basis the detuning and interaction terms are diagonal and the drive flips one atom:

$$\langle b|H|b\rangle=-\delta\sum_ib_i+\sum_{i<j}V_{ij}\,b_ib_j,\qquad \langle b\oplus2^i|H|b\rangle=\tfrac12,\qquad (7)$$

where $b\oplus2^i$ (bitwise exclusive OR) is $b$ with atom $i$ flipped. Each row thus holds one diagonal element and $N$ off-diagonal elements equal to $\Omega/2$. The package builds this matrix directly in compressed sparse row (CSR) format, with $N+1$ stored entries per row, and vectorizes the construction over blocks of $2^{18}$ basis states. The result is identical, element by element, to the historical builder in `study/rydberg_model.py`<!-- source: tests/test_geometry_ed.py::test_builder_equals_study_hamiltonian (np.array_equal on six random chains, N up to 9) -->. At $N=16$ it is built in 0.05 s instead of 0.74 s, and the whole calculation, eigensolver included, peaks at 0.11 GB of memory, whereas the historical builder alone peaks at 0.20 GB<!-- source: validation/results/scaling_benchmark.json: ed[N=16].build_s = 0.0516, peak_rss_GB = 0.110 (build plus eigensolver); study_builder[N=16].build_s = 0.738, peak_rss_GB = 0.201 (build only) -->.

The matrix is real and symmetric. Conjugating it by $\prod_iZ_i$ reverses the sign of every off-diagonal element and leaves the diagonal unchanged, so it is unitarily equivalent to a matrix with non-positive off-diagonal elements in which every pair of basis states is connected by a sequence of single flips. By the Perron–Frobenius theorem [41] the ground state of any finite array with $\Omega\neq0$ is therefore non-degenerate. Ground-state degeneracies of the infinite chain, such as the two $\mathbb{Z}_2$ crystals, appear at finite $N$ as small splittings, which we treat as gaps and follow as functions of $N$.

**Eigensolvers.** For dimensions up to 1024 ($N\le10$) we use dense LAPACK diagonalization (`scipy.linalg.eigh`). For larger $N$ we compute only the few lowest eigenpairs with Krylov-space methods. The Lanczos method [42] builds the space

$$\mathcal K_m(H,v_0)=\operatorname{span}\{v_0,Hv_0,H^2v_0,\dots,H^{m-1}v_0\},\qquad (8)$$

in which $H$ is tridiagonal, and approximates the extreme eigenpairs of $H$ by those of this small projected matrix. Each step costs one sparse matrix-vector product, $O(N2^N)$ operations. We use ARPACK's implicitly restarted Lanczos method [43] through `scipy.sparse.linalg.eigsh` [44], requesting the smallest algebraic eigenvalues with relative tolerance $10^{-12}$, and start from a seeded Gaussian random vector. A symmetric start vector would confine the Krylov space to one symmetry sector and could miss the lowest state of another. The package also offers the locally optimal block preconditioned conjugate gradient method (LOBPCG) [45] with the diagonal preconditioner $(\operatorname{diag}H-\sigma)^{-1}$, $\sigma=\min\operatorname{diag}H-1$, and a block of $k+2$ vectors for $k$ requested states; it falls back to ARPACK if any residual exceeds $10^{-8}\max(1,\lvert E_0\rvert)$. Every eigenpair is returned with its residual $r_k=\lVert Hv_k-E_kv_k\rVert$. The test suite requires the dense, ARPACK and LOBPCG spectra of a 10-atom dimerized chain to agree to $10^{-11}$<!-- source: tests/test_geometry_ed.py::test_eigensolvers_agree (N = 10, eps = 0.3, g = 11.39, Delta = 2.5, k = 3) -->.

The historical finite-chain scans in `study/` used the same approach: dense diagonalization up to dimension 64 and ARPACK (tolerance $10^{-11}$, seeded start vector) above it. Over 6,561 coarse-grid and 312 refinement ground states ($N=4$–12) the largest eigenpair residual is $5.5\times10^{-10}$, dense and sparse energies differ by at most $4.3\times10^{-14}$, and a bundled 62-row regression reference is reproduced to $4.7\times10^{-12}$<!-- source: study/results/extension_summary.json: grid.coarse_ground_states = 6561, grid.extra_refinement_states = 312; checks.maximum_absolute_eigenpair_residual = 5.49e-10; checks.dense_sparse_energy_error = 4.26e-14; checks.prior_csv_matching.maximum_errors (largest: alternating_order_squared = 4.70e-12, 62 points) -->.

**Time evolution.** For dynamics from a stated initial state (in this work the all-ground state $|0\cdots0\rangle$) we compute

**Symmetry-resolved ring ED (preliminary).** Bulk quantities such as critical points and gap ratios are computed on periodic rings, which have no boundary fields. In the four-state pair basis of Sec. 2.3 a ring of $N_p=N/2$ pairs is symmetric under translation by one pair, $T_2$, and under inversion about a strong-bond centre, $I_s$, which maps pair $j$ to pair $-j \bmod N_p$ and exchanges its two atoms. We work at zero momentum of $T_2$ [47]. For each orbit of pair configurations under $T_2$ we keep one representative $|a\rangle$ (the smallest integer label), with orbit length $P_a$, and form

$$|\bar a\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{P_a}}\sum_{t=0}^{P_a-1}T_2^{\,t}|a\rangle,\qquad \langle\bar b|H|\bar a\rangle=\frac{\Omega}{2}\sum_{a\to b'}\sqrt{\frac{P_a}{P_b}}\quad(\bar b\neq\bar a),\qquad (10)$$

By Burnside's lemma the number of orbits is $N_p^{-1}\sum_{t=0}^{N_p-1}d^{\gcd(t,N_p)}\approx d^{N_p}/N_p$ for local dimension $d$. ### 2.3 Exact pair-site regrouping

Grouping the two atoms of each strong pair, $L=2k$ and $R=2k+1$, into one site with the four states $|n_Ln_R\rangle\in\{|00\rangle,|10\rangle,|01\rangle,|11\rangle\}$ rewrites Eq. (2) as

$$\frac{H}{\Omega}=\sum_{k}h_k+\sum_{k<l}\ \sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\{L,R\}}\frac{g}{\big[2(l-k)+o_\beta-o_\alpha\big]^6}\,n_{k\alpha}n_{l\beta},\qquad h_k=\tfrac12\big(X_{kL}+X_{kR}\big)-\delta\big(n_{kL}+n_{kR}\big)+V_s\,n_{kL}n_{kR},\qquad (11)$$

with offsets $o_L=0$ and $o_R=s=1-\varepsilon$. This is a relabelling of the same Hilbert space, not an approximation. It has four practical advantages. (i) The largest coupling, $V_s$ ($96.8\,\Omega$ at $\varepsilon=0.3$, $g=11.39$<!-- source: rydberg_chain.geometry.strong_weak_couplings(0.3, 11.39) = (96.81, 2.36), scratch check, 27 Sept -->), becomes an on-site energy. (ii) The number of sites, and the interaction range measured in sites, are halved. (iii) The pseudo-spin $\tau_k=n_{kL}-n_{kR}$ and the pair-state projectors are on-site operators. (iv) $T_2$ becomes a one-site translation, and $I_s$ a reflection of the site order combined with the on-site exchange $|10\rangle\leftrightarrow|01\rangle$. Pair-site DMRG reproduces atom-site ED to within $10^{-11}\,\Omega$ (Sec. 2.4).

### 2.4 Density-matrix renormalization group

**Matrix product states.** A matrix product state (MPS) on $L$ sites with local dimension $d$ ($d=2$ for atoms, 4 for pairs) is

$$|\psi\rangle=\sum_{s_1,\dots,s_L}A_1^{s_1}A_2^{s_2}\cdots A_L^{s_L}\,|s_1s_2\cdots s_L\rangle,\qquad (12)$$

where each $A_\ell^{s}$ is a matrix of dimension at most $\chi\times\chi$, the bond dimension [8,9,48]. In canonical form, the MPS across the bond after site $\ell$ is a Schmidt decomposition $|\psi\rangle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\chi}\lambda_\alpha|\alpha\rangle_{\text{left}}|\alpha\rangle_{\text{right}}$, so the entanglement entropy of the left block is $S_\ell=-\sum_\alpha\lambda_\alpha^2\log_2\lambda_\alpha^2\le\log_2\chi$. Ground states of gapped one-dimensional Hamiltonians obey an area law [49] and are represented accurately at fixed $\chi$ for any length. At a critical point $S_\ell$ grows logarithmically with $\ell$ (Sec. 2.5), so the required $\chi$ grows as a power of the system size. This is why DMRG reaches hundreds of atoms at modest cost in gapped phases, and why critical points require convergence checks in $\chi$.

**Variational optimization.** DMRG minimizes $E[\psi]=\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle/\langle\psi|\psi\rangle$ over MPS of bond dimension $\chi$. The two-site algorithm sweeps back and forth along the chain. At each bond it solves the eigenvalue problem $H_{\text{eff}}\Theta=E\Theta$ for the two-site tensor $\Theta$ in the basis of the current left and right blocks (by Lanczos), splits $\Theta$ by a singular value decomposition, and keeps the $\chi$ largest singular values. The discarded weight

$$\epsilon_{\text{trunc}}=\sum_{\alpha>\chi}\lambda_\alpha^2\qquad (13)$$

measures the truncation error. Because every step is variational, the DMRG energy is an upper bound, $E_{\text{DMRG}}\ge E_0$. We use the TeNPy library [50,51] with two-site updates, singular values below $10^{-12}$ discarded, and a density-matrix perturbation ("mixer") [52] of amplitude $10^{-5}$ that decays by a factor of two per sweep and is switched off after ten sweeps; the mixer lets the optimization escape poor local minima. A run is declared converged when the energy and the mean bond entropy change between sweeps by less than $10^{-12}$ and $10^{-8}$, respectively, with between 6 and 60 sweeps<!-- source: rydberg_chain/dmrg.py, dmrg_options defaults (chi = 64, max_sweeps = 60, min_sweeps = 6, max_E_err = 1e-12, max_S_err = 1e-8, svd_min = 1e-12, mixer amplitude 1e-5, decay 2, disable_after 10). TeNPy 1.1.1 TwoSiteDMRGEngine.DefaultMixer = DensityMatrixMixer (checked 27 Sept) -->. As an independent check we evaluate the energy variance

$$\sigma_E^2=\langle H^2\rangle-\langle H\rangle^2,\qquad (14)$$

which vanishes for an exact eigenstate. By Temple's inequality [53], $E-E_0\le\sigma_E^2/(E_1-E)$ when $E<E_1$, so a small variance certifies the energy whenever the gap to the next level is not also small.

**Initial state.** DMRG starts from an alternating product state with both end atoms excited. For even $N$ the two halves are joined by a single 00 domain wall at the centre, which the boundary fields of the ordered phase favour (Sec. 3.3).

**Long-range interactions as a matrix product operator.** The Hamiltonian is encoded as a matrix product operator (MPO) that contains every two-body term up to the cutoff explicitly; we do not fit $r^{-6}$ by a sum of exponentials [54,55]. With explicit terms, each MPO bond carries one channel for every operator that has been placed to its left and still awaits its partner. The MPO bond dimension is therefore $D_W=2+(\text{number of distinct coupling operators})\times(\text{range})$: $2+r_{\max}$ on atom sites (one operator, $n$) and $2+2m_{\max}$ on pair sites ($n_L$ and $n_R$). The benchmark MPOs have $D_W=18$ for $m_{\max}=8$ and $D_W=20$ for all pairs in a 10-pair chain<!-- source: validation/results/scaling_benchmark.json, dmrg_pair[*].mpo_bond_dim = 18 (mmax = 8) and 20 (mmax = null, 10 pairs) -->. Because $r^{-6}$ decays quickly, the couplings discarded by $m_{\max}=8$ (atom separations above $17a$) are small: the bound of Eq. (5) is $1.4\times10^{-6}\,\Omega$ for 20 atoms and grows in proportion to $N$, to $2.9\times10^{-4}\,\Omega$ ($1.4\times10^{-6}\,\Omega$ per atom) for 200 atoms<!-- source: checker, 27 Sept: sum of V_ij over atoms in pairs more than 8 apart, rydberg_chain.geometry.coupling_matrix(chain_positions(N, 0.3), 11.39): 1.36e-6 (N = 20), 4.58e-5 (50), 1.26e-4 (100), 2.85e-4 (200). **Finite and infinite DMRG.** All DMRG results in this paper use finite DMRG on open chains, with one site per atom or one site per pair. ### 2.5 Observables and diagnostics

For a state with amplitudes $\psi_b$ in the occupation basis, or an MPS, we compute the following.

*Occupations and density,*

$$\langle n_i\rangle=\sum_b\lvert\psi_b\rvert^2\,b_i,\qquad \bar n=\frac1N\sum_i\langle n_i\rangle.\qquad (15)$$

*Staggered order.* With

$$Q=\frac1N\sum_i(-1)^i\,(2n_i-1),\qquad (16)$$

both perfectly alternating configurations have $Q^2=1$ for either parity of $N$. This corrects the occupation imbalance $M=(2/N)\sum_i(-1)^in_i$ used in earlier versions of the study, whose two ideal values differ in magnitude for odd $N$. For independent sites with excitation probability $p$, $\langle Q^2\rangle=4p(1-p)/N+(2p-1)^2(N\bmod2)/N^2$, so a nonzero $\langle Q^2\rangle$ in a short chain does not by itself indicate order<!-- source: docs/RESULTS_AND_LIMITS.md, "A corrected order diagnostic"; implemented in rydberg_chain/observables.py::staggered_order_squared -->.

*Pair observables.* The pair order $\langle\tau_k\rangle=\langle n_{2k}-n_{2k+1}\rangle$ and the probabilities of the four pair states $|n_Ln_R\rangle$ of each strong pair.

*Structure factors and ring order parameters.* With the pair occupation $\mathcal N_k=n_{kL}+n_{kR}$,

$$S_{\mathcal O}(q)=\frac1{N_p}\Big\langle\Big\lvert\sum_{k=0}^{N_p-1}e^{-iqk}\mathcal O_k\Big\rvert^2\Big\rangle,\qquad \mathcal O\in\{\mathcal N,\tau\}.\qquad (17)$$

Uniform pseudo-spin order ($\mathbb Z_2$ crystal of the dimerized chain, which breaks $I_s$) makes $S_\tau(0)/N_p$ finite in the limit of large $N_p$; a period-two pair density wave (the candidate phase of Sec. 3.2, which would break $T_2$) makes $S_{\mathcal N}(\pi)/N_p$ finite. The constrained-ring script uses the equivalent order parameters $u=N_p^{-1}\sum_k(-1)^k\mathcal N_k$, $\bar\tau=N_p^{-1}\sum_k\tau_k$ and $v=N_p^{-1}\sum_k(-1)^k\tau_k$ and reports $\langle u^2\rangle=S_{\mathcal N}(\pi)/N_p$, $\langle\bar\tau^2\rangle=S_\tau(0)/N_p$ and $\langle v^2\rangle=S_\tau(\pi)/N_p$. *Connected correlations,* $C_{ij}=\langle n_in_j\rangle-\langle n_i\rangle\langle n_j\rangle$.

*Entanglement entropy.* For a bipartition of the chain into a left block $A$ and the rest $B$,

$$S_A=-\operatorname{Tr}\rho_A\log_2\rho_A,\qquad \rho_A=\operatorname{Tr}_B|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|,\qquad (18)$$

measured in bits. For ED we reshape $\psi$ into a $2^{N-\ell}\times2^{\ell}$ matrix, whose singular values are the Schmidt coefficients $\lambda_\alpha$, and use $S_A=-\sum_\alpha\lambda_\alpha^2\log_2\lambda_\alpha^2$; DMRG provides the $\lambda_\alpha$ directly. A two-atom Bell pair gives one bit and a product state zero, which the tests check.

*Central charge from entanglement.* At a critical point described by a conformal field theory (CFT) with central charge $c$, the entropy of the first $\ell$ sites of an open chain of length $L$ is [58]

$$S(\ell)=\frac c6\log_2\!\Big[\frac{2L}{\pi}\sin\frac{\pi\ell}{L}\Big]+s_1+\ldots,\qquad (19)$$

and $S(\ell)=(c/3)\log_2[(L/\pi)\sin(\pi\ell/L)]+s_1'$ for a ring. The omitted terms include oscillations that alternate from bond to bond near open boundaries [59]; in the dimerized chain the planned fits (P7) use cuts of one type only, between pairs, which pair-site DMRG provides directly. The Ising and tricritical Ising CFTs have $c=1/2$ and $7/10$ [60,61].

*Finite-size spectra, critical points and the gap ratio.* On a critical ring of length $L$, the low-lying levels obey [62–64]

$$E_0(L)=e_\infty L-\frac{\pi vc}{6L}+\ldots,\qquad E_n(L)-E_0(L)=\frac{2\pi v}{L}\,x_n+\ldots,\qquad (20)$$

where $v$ is the velocity of excitations and $x_n$ the scaling dimension of the corresponding operator. With dynamic exponent $z=1$, the scaled gap $L\,[E_\sigma(L)-E_0(L)]$ is independent of $L$ at the critical point, so we locate $\Delta_c$ as the crossing of this quantity for two ring sizes, found by Brent's method (requested tolerance $10^{-4}$ in $\Delta/\Omega$; a repeat at $10^{-6}$ changed the $\varepsilon=0.3$ value below by $10^{-6}$). Here $E_\sigma$ is the lowest zero-momentum level that is odd under $I_s$. The velocity cancels in the gap ratio

$$R=\frac{E_\epsilon-E_0}{E_\sigma-E_0}\ \xrightarrow{\,L\to\infty\,}\ \frac{x_\epsilon}{x_\sigma},\qquad (21)$$

where $E_\epsilon$ is the first excited zero-momentum level that is even under $I_s$. ### 2.6 Effective pseudo-spin theory

The analytic picture used in Sec. 3.2 is derived in the theory notes and summarized here. When each strong pair holds at most one excitation, an occupied pair is a two-level system with pseudo-spin $\tau^z=+1$ (left atom excited) or $-1$ (right atom excited). The interaction of excitations in pairs $k$ and $k+m$ decomposes exactly as

$$U_m=A_m+B_m\big(\tau^z_k-\tau^z_{k+m}\big)-J_m\,\tau^z_k\tau^z_{k+m},\qquad J_m=\tfrac14\big[V(2m-s)+V(2m+s)-2V(2m)\big],\qquad B_m=\tfrac14\big[V(2m+s)-V(2m-s)\big],\qquad (22)$$

with $V(r)=g/r^6$ and lengths in units of $a$. Because $r^{-6}$ is convex, $J_m>0$, a ferromagnetic coupling of the pseudo-spins; $J_m\to0$ as $\varepsilon\to1$ ($s\to0$), and the $B_m$ terms cancel in the bulk and leave fields at the chain ends. The drive connects $\tau^z=+1$ and $-1$ at second order through the empty ($|00\rangle$) and doubly excited ($|11\rangle$) pair states, giving the effective transverse-field Ising model

$$H_{\text{eff}}=-\sum_{k,m}J_m\,\tau^z_k\tau^z_{k+m}-h\sum_k\tau^x_k+\ldots,\qquad h\simeq h_0=\frac{\Omega^2}{4}\left[\frac1\Delta+\frac1{V_s-\Delta}\right]\quad(0<\Delta<V_s).\qquad (23)$$

The nearest-neighbour model is critical at $J_1=h$ [57]. The leading-order field $h_0$ neglects the interaction of the flipped excitation with its neighbours in the energy denominators; the dressed form and its accuracy are discussed in Sec. 3.2. ### 2.7 Computational details of modeling in Qiskit

Qiskit [65] provides an independent construction of Eq. (2) and of its time evolution. Substituting Eq. (6) into Eq. (2) gives the Pauli-string form

$$H=c_0\,I+\frac{\Omega}{2}\sum_iX_i+\sum_ih_i^zZ_i+\sum_{i<j}\frac{V_{ij}}{4}Z_iZ_j,\qquad c_0=-\frac{N\Delta}{2}+\frac14\sum_{i<j}V_{ij},\qquad h_i^z=\frac{\Delta}{2}-\frac14\sum_{j\neq i}V_{ij}.\qquad (24)$$

The function `qiskit_hamiltonian` in `study/rydberg_model.py` writes these terms as a `SparsePauliOp`, placing atom $i$ at character $N-1-i$ of each label, and combines duplicate strings. Its matrix equals the occupation-basis matrix of Eq. (7) to $2.2\times10^{-16}$ for a three-atom test chain, and to $3.6\times10^{-15}$ for four-atom chains at three couplings and three interaction cutoffs<!-- source: study/results/summary.json, checks.Pauli_vs_bit_basis_H_error = 2.2e-16 (3 atoms, V_nn = 3, Omega = 0.7, Delta = 0.4 in the code's energy units); study/results/extension_summary.json, checks.qiskit_operator_max_entry_error = 3.6e-15 (study/run_extension.py: N = 4, (g, delta) = (2, -1.5), (8, 1), (16, 5), cutoffs nearest1, nearest2, all) -->.

**Exact evolution.** `HamiltonianGate(H, t)` appends the unitary $e^{-iHt}$, obtained by dense matrix exponentiation, to a circuit, which we simulate as a state vector (`Statevector`). For two atoms at $V/\Omega=0$ and 20, sampled at 161 times in $0\le\Omega t\le2\pi$, these states agree with the SciPy evolution of Eq. (9) to $1.0\times10^{-14}$<!-- source: study/results/summary.json, checks.qiskit_scipy_state_error_V0 = 1.02e-14, checks.qiskit_scipy_state_error_V20 = 5.2e-15 -->. The entanglement entropy of a random five-atom state computed by our code agrees with Qiskit's `partial_trace` and `entropy` to $2.2\times10^{-16}$ bits, and a two-qubit Bell circuit gives 1.0000 bit<!-- source: study/results/summary.json, checks.entropy_vs_Qiskit_error = 2.2e-16; checks.bell_entropy_bits = 1.0000000000000002 -->.

**Split-step circuits.** To represent the evolution with standard gates we split $H=H_X+H_D$ into the drive $H_X=(\Omega/2)\sum_iX_i$ and the diagonal part $H_D=-\Delta\sum_in_i+\sum_{i<j}V_{ij}n_in_j$, and use the symmetric second-order (Strang) product [66–68]

$$U_n=\Big[e^{-iH_X\,dt/2}\,e^{-iH_D\,dt}\,e^{-iH_X\,dt/2}\Big]^n,\qquad dt=\frac tn,\qquad \big\lVert U_n-e^{-iHt}\big\rVert=O\big(t\,dt^2\big).\qquad (25)$$

The prefactor of the error is set by nested commutators of $H_X$ and $H_D$ [69]. Each factor is exact in gates:

$$e^{-iH_X\,dt/2}=\prod_i R_X^{(i)}\!\Big(\frac{\Omega\,dt}{2}\Big),\qquad e^{-iH_D\,dt}=\prod_iP^{(i)}(\Delta\,dt)\prod_{i<j}CP^{(ij)}(-V_{ij}\,dt),\qquad (26)$$

with $R_X(\theta)=e^{-i\theta X/2}$, $P(\lambda)=\operatorname{diag}(1,e^{i\lambda})$ and $CP(\lambda)=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,1,e^{i\lambda})$. The diagonal factor is exact because all terms of $H_D$ commute. One step uses $2N$ $R_X$ gates, $N$ phase gates and one controlled-phase gate per retained pair: 18 gates for four atoms with all pairs<!-- source: study/results/circuit_convergence.csv, gates = 180 for 10 steps (N = 4, all 6 pairs); stepped_circuit in study/rydberg_model.py -->.

**Scope.** Qiskit serves as an independent check of the operator, the bit ordering, the sign of the detuning and the time evolution for two to five atoms. **Scope and limitations of the methods.** All calculations treat the ideal, coherent Hamiltonian of Eq. ### 3.1 How the physics varies with the coupling constant

The earlier calculations in `study/` (full Hilbert space, all pair interactions, $\hbar=\Omega=a=1$; dynamics at $\Delta=0$ from $|g\cdots g\rangle$) reproduce established physics; here they validate the solvers and separate the effects of $g$ from boundary effects.

### 3.2 How the physics varies with alternating coupling constants

We now alternate the gaps, with $s=a(1-\varepsilon)$ inside each strong pair and $w=a(1+\varepsilon)$ between pairs (Eq. 4). The mean spacing stays $a$, so $g=(R_b/a)^6$ has the same meaning as in Sec. 3.1. All numbers in this section come from exploratory pilot scripts, several of them in restricted bases, and are preliminary; each is tied to the computation of the research plan (P0–P9) that must reproduce it.

#### 3.2.1 Symmetry and the pair pseudo-spin

Dimerization removes translation by one atom. The chain keeps translation by one pair, $T_2$, inversion about a strong-bond centre, $I_s$, and $I_w=T_2I_s$. A strong pair holding one excitation has two classical states, left ($\tau^z=+1$) and right ($\tau^z=-1$), which $I_s$ exchanges. Occupied pairs $m$ pair spacings apart couple through Eq. (22). $J_m$ is a second difference of the convex function $r^{-6}$, so it is positive (ferromagnetic). It vanishes as $\varepsilon\to1$, where the distances $2ma$ and $2ma\pm s$ merge. For neighbouring pairs, $J_1=\tfrac14[V(w)+V(2a+s)-2V(2a)]$, and the repulsion $V(2a)$ between aligned excitations reduces it: at $g=11.39$ and $\varepsilon=0.6$, $J_1$ is 44% smaller than $V(w)/4$, the value it would have if only the weak-bond interaction counted<!-- source: docs/DIMERIZED_THEORY.md section 2 ("about 44%"); scratch rerun, 27 Sept (research/pilots/2026-09-27/drafting_checks/eff_check.py): J_1 = 0.0956, V(w)/4 = 0.1697, reduction 43.6% -->. The $B_m$ terms leave only boundary fields, which force a domain wall into even open chains (Sec. 3.3.3); bulk quantities are therefore computed on rings. The drive flips $\tau$ at second order, acting as a transverse field $h_0>\Omega^2/(4\Delta)$ (Eq. 23) that stays finite as $\varepsilon\to1$. In this picture, every crystal whose occupied pairs carry a free left/right choice must lose its $\tau$ order before $\varepsilon$ reaches 1; what happens then depends on the symmetries the crystal breaks.

#### 3.2.3 Even and odd crystal periods (preliminary)

At $R_b/a=3.3$ the uniform chain has four degenerate period-4 crystals [1], permuted by the group $D_4$, which melt through a chiral transition or an incommensurate floating phase [12,14]. Dimerization leaves only two commuting twofold symmetries, $T_2$ and $I_s$, which generate the Klein group $\mathbb{Z}_2\times\mathbb{Z}_2$. The four patterns remain degenerate, but no remaining symmetry cycles through all of them. The order parameter therefore splits into a part $u$, which records the occupied sublattice of pairs and is odd under $T_2$, and the pseudo-spin $\tau$, odd under $I_s$, and the two can order separately. A pair-density wave (PDW), with $u$ ordered and $\tau$ disordered, can then separate the $\mathbb{Z}_4$ crystal from the disordered phase; in it, every other strong pair holds one excitation shared between its atoms. The PDW breaks $T_2$ and preserves $I_s$, so it would be a distinct phase, and the transition at which $\tau$ orders within it is controlled by $J_2$ against $h$. It resembles the rung-symmetric $\mathbb{Z}_p^+$ phases of two-leg ladders [34,35]. One difference is that $\tau$ is reversed by a spatial inversion rather than by an internal leg exchange, so a derivative (Huse–Fisher chiral [73]) coupling between $u$ and the $I_s$-odd components of the order parameter is symmetry-allowed near $\varepsilon=0$<!-- checker, 27 Sept: with v = (1/N_p) sum_k (-1)^k (n_kL - n_kR) (odd under T2 and I_s; tau = u v in a perfect pattern), the term u dv/dx - v du/dx is invariant under T2 (u, v -> -u, -v) and under I_s (x -> -x, v -> -v). The earlier wording implied that ladders forbid chiral terms; the ladder abstracts (arXiv:2411.05494, 2412.11201) report Z4-chiral and Z_p^+ chiral transitions, so no contrast is claimed. docs/DIMERIZED_THEORY.md section 4 and 06_discussion.md ("Ladders and tricritical points") use the older wording. -->. In the $\mathbb{Z}_3$ regime ($R_b/a=2.7$), $T_2$ still cycles the three patterns and $I_s$ exchanges two, so $D_3$ survives and no new transition is expected.

Pilot ED on rings in a constrained basis (Sec. 2.2) is consistent with both predictions. #### 3.2.4 A negative test for tricriticality

The research plan fixed a negative test before the production runs: dimerization alone should not make the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ line tricritical. For $\Delta<V_s$ each strong pair is a three-state superatom (empty, $L$ or $R$). For $\Omega\to0$ the chain then maps onto a spin-1 Blume–Emery–Griffiths chain [74], an Ising model with bilinear and biquadratic couplings, with $S_k=0$ on an empty pair and $S_k=\tau_k$ on an occupied one. Writing the interaction of neighbouring pairs as $-J_1S_kS_{k+1}-KS_k^2S_{k+1}^2$ (plus boundary terms), Eq. (22) gives the bilinear coupling $J_1$ and the biquadratic coupling $K=-A_1=-[V(2a)+J_1]$, so that

$$\frac{K}{J_1}=-1-\frac{V(2a)}{J_1}.\qquad(29)$$

A tricritical point ends a first-order line. On the Blume–Capel route [75,76] this is a direct jump from the empty to the ordered state, which needs a net attraction between aligned neighbouring occupied pairs. These pairs are always $2a$ apart, whatever $\varepsilon$, and repel with $V(2a)>0$. Dimerization lowers $J_1$ while $V(2a)$ stays fixed, so $K/J_1$ moves further from the attractive side. The pilot numerics agree. The pseudo-spin picture explains the difference. The $B_m$ terms of Eq. (22) cancel in the bulk and leave end fields that favour $\tau=+1$ on the first pair and $\tau=-1$ on the last, that is, excitation of both end atoms, which is also seen in experiments [1,78]. With a whole number of pairs no uniform $\tau$ satisfies both ends, so the ground state holds one wall from $\tau=+1$ to $\tau=-1$: a 00 bond between pairs (the opposite wall would be a blockaded 11 bond). The end fields outweigh the wall's ferromagnetic cost: excitations on opposite sides of the wall are $2ma+s$ rather than $2ma$ apart, so the wall lowers the classical energy (by $0.117\,\Omega$ for $N=8$, $g=8$, $\Delta/\Omega=3$ and $\Omega=0$)<!-- source: scratch check, 27 Sept (research/pilots/2026-09-27/drafting_checks/derived.py): N = 8, all pairs, Omega = 0, Delta = 3: E(10100101) - E(10101010) = -0.1170 (-0.1521 at eps = 0.3, g = 11.39, Delta = 2.5). $$p_q(k)=\frac2P\sin^2\frac{qk\pi}{P},\qquad k=1,\dots,P-1.\qquad(31)$$

## 4. Discussion

**What is new.** Subject to the literature check (P0) and to the validation computations (P1–P3), three results go beyond the uniform chain. First, an exact rewriting of the $r^{-6}$ interaction between singly occupied strong pairs (Eq. 22) turns dimerization into a competition between two quantities. One is a ferromagnetic coupling $J_m$ between pseudo-spins, which record whether the left or the right atom of a pair is excited; it vanishes as $\varepsilon\to1$. The other is a transverse field induced by the laser, which does not vanish. Second, the symmetries that survive dimerization decide what happens to each crystal. The $\mathbb{Z}_2$ crystal melts through a single Ising line. The $\mathbb{Z}_4$ crystal can melt in two steps, through a pair-density wave (PDW), and the pilot spectra point that way. The $\mathbb{Z}_3$ crystal keeps its threefold degeneracy. This even/odd rule rests on constrained bases and small rings and remains preliminary. Third, the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ line is located quantitatively. ## 5. Conclusion

In a laser-driven van der Waals Rydberg chain with alternating spacing, the pseudo-spin of each strong pair, coupled ferromagnetically through a second difference of $r^{-6}$ and flipped by the laser, organizes the preliminary results. These are a single Ising melting line of the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ crystal, which a dressed transverse-field Ising model reproduces to 1% at $\varepsilon=0.6$ and 0.7; a candidate pair-density wave between the $\mathbb{Z}_4$ crystal and the disordered phase; a $\mathbb{Z}_3$ degeneracy that survives dimerization; and no tricritical point for $R_b/a=1.30$–1.85 and $\varepsilon\le0.7$. The $\mathbb{Z}_2$ line is the firmest result and still needs recomputation in the full pair basis (P3). The even/odd rule becomes a finding only after validation in the full basis and on larger systems (P1, P2, P7).
