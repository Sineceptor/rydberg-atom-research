### 3.2 How the physics varies with alternating coupling constants

We now alternate the gaps, with $s=a(1-\varepsilon)$ inside each strong pair and $w=a(1+\varepsilon)$ between pairs (Eq. 2.3). The mean spacing stays $a$, so $g=(R_b/a)^6$ has the same meaning as in Sec. 3.1. All numbers in this section come from exploratory pilot scripts, several of them in restricted bases, and are preliminary; each is tied to the computation of the research plan (P0–P9) that must reproduce it.

#### 3.2.1 Symmetry and the pair pseudo-spin

Dimerization removes translation by one atom. The chain keeps translation by one pair, $T_2$, inversion about a strong-bond centre, $I_s$, and $I_w=T_2I_s$. A strong pair holding one excitation has two classical states, left ($\tau^z=+1$) and right ($\tau^z=-1$), which $I_s$ exchanges. Occupied pairs $m$ pair spacings apart couple through Eq. (2.21). $J_m$ is a second difference of the convex function $r^{-6}$, so it is positive (ferromagnetic). It vanishes as $\varepsilon\to1$, where the distances $2ma$ and $2ma\pm s$ merge. For neighbouring pairs, $J_1=\tfrac14[V(w)+V(2a+s)-2V(2a)]$, and the repulsion $V(2a)$ between aligned excitations reduces it: at $g=11.39$ and $\varepsilon=0.6$, $J_1$ is 44% smaller than $V(w)/4$, the value it would have if only the weak-bond interaction counted<!-- source: docs/DIMERIZED_THEORY.md section 2 ("about 44%"); scratch rerun, 27 Sept (eff_check.py): J_1 = 0.0956, V(w)/4 = 0.1697, reduction 43.6% -->. The $B_m$ terms leave only boundary fields, which force a domain wall into even open chains (Sec. 3.3.3); bulk quantities are therefore computed on rings. The drive flips $\tau$ at second order, acting as a transverse field $h_0>\Omega^2/(4\Delta)$ (Eq. 2.22) that stays finite as $\varepsilon\to1$. In this picture, every crystal whose occupied pairs carry a free left/right choice must lose its $\tau$ order before $\varepsilon$ reaches 1; what happens then depends on the symmetries the crystal breaks.

The leading-order condition $J_1=h_0$ underestimates $\Delta_c$ by 63% at $\varepsilon=0.3$ and still by 7.6% at 0.7. Its energy denominators ignore the fact that removing an excitation also removes its interaction with its neighbours. In a crystal with all excitations on left atoms, that interaction is $I_L=2V(2a)$ for a left excitation and $I_R=V(w)+V(2a+s)$ for an excitation moved to the right atom. Including these interactions gives the dressed field

$$h\simeq\frac{\Omega^2}{8}\left[\frac{1}{\Delta-I_L}+\frac{1}{\Delta-I_R}\right]+\frac{\Omega^2}{4\,(V_s-\Delta)},\qquad(3.2)$$

which reproduces $\Delta_c$ to 0.9% at $\varepsilon=0.6$ and to 0.5% at 0.7. Equation (3.2) is empirical. A Schrieffer–Wolff derivation [1], which removes the high-energy pair states order by order in $\Omega$, is planned in P4; at the same order it renormalizes $J_m$ and generates a symmetry-allowed term that couples $\tau^x_k$ to $\tau^z_{k\pm1}$. The error of Eq. (3.2) grows to 15% at $\varepsilon=0.45$, and at 0.3 the model fails. On the disordered side of the line at strong dimerization, where $\Delta$ is positive but below $\Delta_c$, each strong pair holds about one excitation shared between its atoms, $a|00\rangle+b(|10\rangle+|01\rangle)/\sqrt2$. This is not a separate phase. The drive breaks excitation-number conservation, so there is no U(1) symmetry, and the shared-pair state is $I_s$-even, like the weakly occupied disordered state; no symmetry separates the two, and the change between them is a crossover. This differs from the dimerized chain of Zeybek et al. [2], which has two Rydberg levels coupled by dipolar exchange and no drive, so that the number of excitations is conserved; there a bond-order phase with one excitation shared on each dimer appears at half filling. Figure 8a will show the line at three couplings (P3), and Fig. 9 a systematic test of Eq. (3.2) (P4).

#### 3.2.3 Even and odd crystal periods (preliminary)

At $R_b/a=3.3$ the uniform chain has four degenerate period-4 crystals [3], permuted by the group $D_4$, which melt through a chiral transition or an incommensurate floating phase [4,5]. Dimerization leaves only two commuting twofold symmetries, $T_2$ and $I_s$, which generate the Klein group $\mathbb{Z}_2\times\mathbb{Z}_2$. The four patterns remain degenerate, but no remaining symmetry cycles through all of them. The order parameter therefore splits into a part $u$, which records the occupied sublattice of pairs and is odd under $T_2$, and the pseudo-spin $\tau$, odd under $I_s$, and the two can order separately. A pair-density wave (PDW), with $u$ ordered and $\tau$ disordered, can then separate the $\mathbb{Z}_4$ crystal from the disordered phase; in it, every other strong pair holds one excitation shared between its atoms. The PDW breaks $T_2$ and preserves $I_s$, so it would be a distinct phase, and the transition at which $\tau$ orders within it is controlled by $J_2$ against $h$. It resembles the rung-symmetric $\mathbb{Z}_p^+$ phases of two-leg ladders [6,7]. One difference is that $\tau$ is reversed by a spatial inversion rather than by an internal leg exchange, so a derivative (Huse–Fisher chiral [8]) coupling between $u$ and the $I_s$-odd components of the order parameter is symmetry-allowed near $\varepsilon=0$<!-- checker, 27 Sept: with v = (1/N_p) sum_k (-1)^k (n_kL - n_kR) (odd under T2 and I_s; tau = u v in a perfect pattern), the term u dv/dx - v du/dx is invariant under T2 (u, v -> -u, -v) and under I_s (x -> -x, v -> -v). The earlier wording implied that ladders forbid chiral terms; the ladder abstracts (arXiv:2411.05494, 2412.11201) report Z4-chiral and Z_p^+ chiral transitions, so no contrast is claimed. docs/DIMERIZED_THEORY.md section 4 and 06_discussion.md ("Ladders and tricritical points") use the older wording. -->. In the $\mathbb{Z}_3$ regime ($R_b/a=2.7$), $T_2$ still cycles the three patterns and $I_s$ exchanges two, so $D_3$ survives and no new transition is expected.

Pilot ED on rings in a constrained basis (Sec. 2.2) is consistent with both predictions. At $R_b/a=3.3$ and $\Delta/\Omega=3$ (Table 2), the inversion-odd gap closes faster than $1/N$ for $\varepsilon\le0.2$, scales as $1/N$ near $\varepsilon=0.3$, and saturates for $\varepsilon\ge0.4$. For $\varepsilon\ge0.2$ the two lowest states are $I_s$-even and have $T_2=\pm1$. #### 3.2.4 A negative test for tricriticality

The research plan fixed a negative test before the production runs: dimerization alone should not make the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ line tricritical. For $\Delta<V_s$ each strong pair is a three-state superatom (empty, $L$ or $R$). For $\Omega\to0$ the chain then maps onto a spin-1 Blume–Emery–Griffiths chain [9], an Ising model with bilinear and biquadratic couplings, with $S_k=0$ on an empty pair and $S_k=\tau_k$ on an occupied one. Writing the interaction of neighbouring pairs as $-J_1S_kS_{k+1}-KS_k^2S_{k+1}^2$ (plus boundary terms), Eq. (2.21) gives the bilinear coupling $J_1$ and the biquadratic coupling $K=-A_1=-[V(2a)+J_1]$, so that

$$\frac{K}{J_1}=-1-\frac{V(2a)}{J_1}.\qquad(3.3)$$

A tricritical point ends a first-order line. On the Blume–Capel route [10,11] this is a direct jump from the empty to the ordered state, which needs a net attraction between aligned neighbouring occupied pairs. These pairs are always $2a$ apart, whatever $\varepsilon$, and repel with $V(2a)>0$. Dimerization lowers $J_1$ while $V(2a)$ stays fixed, so $K/J_1$ moves further from the attractive side. The pilot numerics agree. At the largest rings the gap ratio lies between 7.91 and 8.15 for $R_b/a=1.30$–1.85 and $\varepsilon\le0.7$, near the Ising value 8 and far from the tricritical Ising value 8/3; where it deviates most, it approaches 8 as the ring grows. Two further runs at $R_b/a=2.15$ and 2.31, where neighbouring pairs lie inside the blockade radius and the melting need not be a direct disordered–$\mathbb{Z}_2$ transition, give $R=8.7$ and 10.0 at 20 atoms, still drifting with size; they are outside this test and will be revisited in P3<!-- source: largest ring of each run. research/pilots/2026-09-27/outputs/crit_*.json, R_b of the largest size pair: 7.915 (g = 4.83, eps = 0.45) to 7.998 (g = 11.39, eps = 0.3); results.jsonl, N10 (20 atoms) ratio: 7.926 (g = 11.39, eps = 0.6) to 8.147 (g = 40, eps = 0.3). g = 40, eps = 0.3: 8.323, 8.190, 8.147 at 12, 16, 20 atoms.
